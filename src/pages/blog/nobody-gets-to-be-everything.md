> To anyone I’ve offended, I just want to say, I reinvented electric cars and I’m sending people to Mars in a rocket ship. Did you think I was also going to be a chill, normal dude?
>
> <footer>— Elon Musk, <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fCF8I_X1qKI"><cite>Saturday Night Live</cite>, 2021</a>. Opening epigraph to Walter Isaacson's <cite>Elon Musk</cite>.</footer>

I have noticed something strange about the way we talk about impressive people.

Someone is brilliant at what they do. They have built something remarkable, mastered a difficult craft, changed an industry, written books that outlive them, won at a level almost nobody else can reach, or simply developed one ability so far beyond the normal range that you cannot honestly look away.

And after a few minutes of admiration, the corrections begin. If only he were more approachable. If only she knew how to talk to people. If only he were not so obsessive. She is talented, but difficult. He is good, but would it kill him to be nicer? Imagine how much further she would go if she worked on her personality.

Maybe. Sometimes the criticism is completely fair. Talent does not excuse cruelty, achievement does not cancel abuse, and being excellent at one thing does not buy anybody the right to make other people's lives miserable.

But I think there is another possibility we rarely consider: what if the person is unusually good in one area partly because they are unusually bad, limited, or uninterested in another?

What if these things are not separate settings we can adjust independently? The obsession that made the work excellent may be the same obsession that makes the person unavailable. The [refusal to accept mediocre work](/blog/good-enough-is-the-enemy-of-great) may be connected to the impatience everybody complains about. The person capable of sitting alone with a problem for ten hours may never become the person charming every stranger at a party. We keep asking for the strength without its shadow and the gift without its cost, as though an extraordinary person should also be perfectly pleasant. Nobody gets to be everything.

## We review people like unfinished products

There is something almost consumerist about the way we judge people. We encounter a human being with one rare and astonishing quality, then immediately begin listing the features we wish they would add: brilliant, but awkward; disciplined, but rigid; confident, but arrogant; kind, but not ambitious; ambitious, but not present enough; independent, but distant; generous, but terrible with money; careful, but slow; decisive, but impatient.

It never ends because the imaginary person in our head has no trade-offs. They are bold without ever being reckless, gentle without ever being weak, driven without neglecting anything, confident without offending anybody, social without being distracted, intelligent without being intimidating, and successful without making us feel small.

That person does not exist.

More importantly, we do not demand this completeness from everybody. We rarely meet a lovely, socially graceful person and say, "Yes, she is kind, but if only she could revolutionize physics." We do not look at the pleasant colleague everybody enjoys having lunch with and complain that he has failed to build a global company.

That would sound absurdly cruel. We understand that kindness is valuable by itself. We understand that being a good friend may be the great thing that person brings into the world.

But when someone brings an exceptional form of excellence, we suddenly treat excellence as the beginning of the checklist instead of one enormous item already checked.

The reward for doing one impossible thing is that everybody starts asking why you have not done the rest.

## Sometimes the weakness and the strength are the same thing

We like to imagine personality as a control panel. Turn up the creativity. Turn down the awkwardness. Keep the ambition, reduce the intensity, add some warmth, remove the stubbornness, preserve the standards, and make the person easier to work with.

But a person is not assembled from independent sliders, and the same trait changes its name depending on whether we like what it is doing for us. When the result is good, we call it conviction; when it inconveniences us, stubbornness. When it produces exceptional work, it is attention to detail; when the deadline moves, perfectionism. A person who resists social pressure and turns out to be right is independent, but when they resist us, they are difficult. Sensitivity producing beautiful art is a gift until the same sensitivity makes ordinary life harder, when it suddenly becomes fragility.

Psychology has some evidence for this intuition. Adam Grant and Barry Schwartz reviewed what they called the ["too much of a good thing" effect](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26162116/): traits and practices we consider positive can reach a point where more of them stops helping and begins to hurt. The useful thing and the costly thing are not always opposites. Sometimes the cost is the useful thing pushed too far or used in the wrong context.

Research on creativity also complicates our desire for the perfectly rounded person. A meta-analytic review of group creativity research notes that creative people tend to be lower in agreeableness, even though agreeableness helps trust, morale, and team performance. It also points to a tension between the social stimulation that can feed creativity and the quiet reflection required to develop original ideas. Different valuable outcomes can ask for [different arrangements of personality](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12413614/).

None of this means a disagreeable person is secretly a genius. Plenty of difficult people are just difficult. It also does not mean creative people cannot be kind. Human beings are more complicated than a personality test and history contains generous geniuses as well as unbearable fools.

The point is smaller and, I think, more useful: you cannot assume that every admirable quality can be maximized in the same person without friction. Sometimes improving one thing changes another thing you did not realize was connected to it.

## Obsession is selective apathy

Obsession is one of those words we use as both praise and warning. We say someone is obsessed when ordinary interest no longer explains their behaviour, when a subject has seized enough of their attention that the rest of life must organize itself around it. Used carelessly, the word glamorizes compulsion and self-destruction. Used accurately, it describes an unequal distribution of concern.

Some people seem to live at only two temperatures: obsession and apathy. If a subject matters to them, it receives everything—their time, memory, competitive instinct, curiosity, and capacity for repetition. If it does not matter, persuasion barely moves them. From the outside, the apathy looks like a separate defect. Often it is simply the negative space created by the obsession. The attention has already gone somewhere else.

Bill Gates is a useful example. He has described himself between the ages of eighteen and thirty as [fanatically focused on Microsoft](https://www.economicclub.org/sites/default/files/transcripts/Bill%20Gates%20Final%20Transcript.pdf). During the [eight-week push to create BASIC for the Altair](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1994/01/10/e-mail-from-bill), he and Paul Allen entered a coding binge in which Gates reportedly fell asleep at the keyboard, dreamed about code, woke up, and resumed with almost no transition between sleep and work. The code was not one important interest among several. For that period, it became the organizing fact of his existence.

Years later, Gates admitted that his obsession with work meant he [did not invest enough in friendships](https://time.com/4652551/bill-gates-warren-buffett/) in his twenties and thirties. That admission tells us more than another story about long hours. It shows the actual mechanism of the see-saw. The same life contained extraordinary attention and costly neglect; software received the obsession, while other parts of life received what looked like apathy.

This is not a command to become obsessed, and it is not proof that obsession always rewards its owner. Many people ruin their health, relationships, and judgment while producing nothing of value. An obsession can be badly chosen, poorly governed, or aimed at something too small to justify its appetite. It can also become an excuse for forcing everyone nearby to accommodate behaviour the obsessed person refuses to examine.

But we should stop pretending that exceptional concentration resembles healthy enthusiasm with slightly longer hours. Obsession narrows. It excludes. It makes a person powerful in one direction by making much of the surrounding world feel faint. The strategic question is not whether obsession is good or bad in the abstract. It is [whether the object is worthy](/blog/happiness-is-overrated), whether the cost is understood, and whether the obsessed person is paying that cost instead of quietly transferring it to everyone else.

## History is full of uneven people

Isaac Newton gave us a new account of motion and gravity, helped build calculus, transformed optics, and wrote one of the most important scientific works in history.

He was also, according to the University of Oxford's [Newton Project](https://newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/his-personal-life), deeply introverted, fiercely private, insecure, prone to depression and violent temper, and relentless toward people he believed had threatened him. His conflict with Gottfried Leibniz over calculus became bitter and personal. Newton did not merely want to establish his claim. He helped conduct a campaign against Leibniz's reputation.

It would be foolish to say Newton discovered gravity because he was vindictive. History cannot prove something that neat, and human beings do not fit into moral equations so easily.

But it would be equally foolish to imagine that Newton's extraordinary capacity for solitary concentration, secrecy, suspicion, intellectual combat, and refusal to let go all lived in separate rooms. The mind that could stay with a problem long after other people moved on was also a mind that could stay with a grievance long after it had become ugly.

Florence Nightingale presents another version of the same problem. She changed nursing, hospital design, public health, and the use of statistics in public policy. Even while living with long periods of illness, she produced an astonishing body of work and pushed reforms through institutions that did not want to move.

She was also difficult. A medical history published for her bicentenary describes her letters as moving between [criticism, charm, praise, and passive aggression](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7252134/) in order to get results. She could exhaust the people around her. She applied pressure because pressure moved things.

Would a softer Nightingale have been easier to live with? Almost certainly. Would that version of her have forced the same reforms through the British establishment? Maybe. But history does not give us that version. It gives us the whole, uneven person.

Steve Jobs is a more recent and more familiar example. People who worked with him describe the same intensity from two directions. Guy Kawasaki called him ["brilliant and difficult"](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/guy-kawasaki-lessons-learned-steve-jobs), a man whose vision pushed people toward excellent work and whose temper could humiliate them. The refusal to accept what other people considered good enough helped Apple make extraordinary products. It also hurt people.

We do not have to clean that story up. The products do not make the cruelty kind. The cruelty does not make the products ordinary. Both facts can remain true at once.

Michael Jordan's competitiveness worked in a similar way. The NBA's own history describes him as [ultra-demanding of teammates](https://www.nba.com/news/history-nba-legend-michael-jordan), while Phil Jackson remembered that Jordan could be harsh and confrontational. That standard helped define a team that won six championships, but Scottie Pippen's encouragement and communication mattered too. Jordan's edge was powerful. It was not sufficient by itself, and it was not pleasant for everybody who encountered it.

These stories are not proof that greatness requires bad character. They are proof that greatness can arrive inside a badly balanced human being. History has never waited for complete people before allowing them to do important work, so it is strange that we keep insisting on completeness before deciding how much of their work we are willing to respect.

## The see-saw

I think life is more like a see-saw than a checklist.

To push one side unusually high, some weight often gathers on the other side. Time spent mastering a craft is time not spent somewhere else. Attention is finite. Energy is finite. Even the emotional habits that help in one environment can become liabilities in another.

This is the uncomfortable arithmetic of excellence: to become truly good at ABC, you may have to accept being bad at XYZ. Not morally bad, cruel, dishonest, or irresponsible, but simply underdeveloped, average, or unavailable in the part of life that did not receive the hours because the hours went somewhere else.

To become a world-class athlete, you may have to be bad at spontaneous living. Your meals, sleep, travel, and evenings cannot belong entirely to the mood of the moment.

To [build a company](/blog/its-easier-to-be-an-employee), you may have to be bad at keeping the same relaxed schedule as your friends. To master a solitary craft, you may have to miss enough social events that some people stop inviting you. To become deeply present with your children, you may have to be less available to your career. To be the friend who is always there, you may have to accept that some private ambition will grow more slowly.

You can debate each choice and decide that the trade is foolish, unhealthy, or simply not for you, but you [cannot make the trade disappear](/blog/do-you-want-it-done-or-checked-off). Every serious yes contains several noes: five hours of training remove five hours from something else; rewriting the chapter again may refuse sleep, entertainment, another project, or somebody's invitation; protecting your family may reject the promotion that demands your permanent absence; being liked by everybody may prevent the unpopular decision your work requires.

This is why "balance" can become a dishonest word. Sometimes we use it to pretend every part of life can receive first place at the same time. It cannot. If everything is a priority, nothing has actually been chosen.

Greatness is not only built from what a person repeatedly does. It is also shaped by what they are willing to neglect, postpone, disappoint, refuse, and remain ordinary at.

The founder who moves with unreasonable speed may be impatient with slower people, just as the artist who notices what everybody else misses may be unable to stop noticing. A scientist comfortable enough with solitude to spend years on one question may be awkward in rooms where social ease matters more than depth. The leader willing to make a decision everybody hates may not always know when listening would be the braver act; the kind person who gives everybody another chance may struggle to fire the person destroying the team; the friend who is always available may fail to protect enough time for their own work. Every quality has a direction in which it becomes expensive.

And this is not only about famous people. All of us are making these trades, whether we admit it or not. Some people optimize for peace. Some for achievement. Some for family. Some for freedom. Some for status. Some for craft. Some for being loved by everybody in the room.

Then we meet someone who chose a different side of the see-saw and call their trade-off a defect.

Often what we really mean is not, "This person is bad." What we mean is, "This person's arrangement does not make my experience of them comfortable."

Those are not the same judgment.

## Extraordinary results often come from unreasonable routines

There is another mistake we make when we look at exceptional people. We admire an outcome produced by a routine we would never agree to live.

Arnold Schwarzenegger did not become Arnold Schwarzenegger by having a healthy interest in exercise. In a 2017 commencement address, he described [finding his blueprint in the bodybuilder Reg Park](/blog/you-want-originality-too-early) and then [training five hours a day for five years](https://time.com/4779796/arnold-schwarzenegger-university-of-houston-uh-commencement-graduation/) until he became the youngest Mr. Universe.

Five hours a day is insane to most people, which is precisely the point. The ordinary routine was available to everybody, and the ordinary routine could not produce that body. When he moved toward acting, he attacked the new weakness with the same mentality: acting lessons, English lessons, voice lessons, speech lessons, and accent-removal lessons. He became the person we recognize because he was willing to organize his life around [actions repeated every day](/blog/resolutions-fail) at a level most people would find excessive.

You can dislike the extremity. You can decide that no title, body, or career is worth arranging your life that way. But you cannot honestly want the result while quietly deleting the regime that produced it.

Kobe Bryant carried a similar appetite into basketball. Players described arriving for 6 a.m. practice only to discover that Bryant had already been completing an intense private workout for ninety minutes. Even late in his career, younger NBA players recalled him pushing through [two-and-a-half or three-hour conditioning sessions](https://www.nba.com/blazers/forwardcenter/players-coaches-share-their-thoughts-career-legacy-kobe-bryant) and beating them in sprints.

Toni Morrison's version was quieter but no less revealing. She began [writing before dawn](https://www.theparisreview.org/interviews/1888/the-art-of-fiction-no-134-toni-morrison) because she had small children and needed to work before they woke and called for her. She was not handed a peaceful writer's life with empty afternoons. She found the hour her life had left unclaimed and built her work inside it.

These routines are not instructions. Training like Arnold without Arnold's body, coaching, recovery, or goals may simply injure you. Copying Kobe's hours does not give you Kobe's talent. Waking at 4 a.m. does not make your sentences Toni Morrison's.

The lesson is not that suffering automatically produces greatness. It is that extraordinary work usually asks for an arrangement of attention, repetition, and sacrifice that looks unreasonable to people who do not want the same result badly enough to pay for it. You do not have to want it, but if you reject the price, be honest that you are also rejecting the product. Do not ask another person to produce an extreme life through moderate commitment, then criticize the intensity that made the result possible.

## Discomfort is not harm

There has to be a line here, because this idea becomes dangerous when people use it to excuse anything a talented person does.

Social awkwardness is not abuse. Needing solitude is not cruelty. Being blunt is not automatically contempt. Forgetting birthdays is not the same as humiliating employees. Refusing small talk is not the same as refusing responsibility for the damage you cause.

We should stop treating every inconvenience as a character emergency. But we should also stop romanticizing harm as the necessary price of genius.

Some traits remain a definite no. Cruelty is not intensity, violence is not passion, humiliation is not high standards, dishonesty is not creativity, exploitation is not ambition, and refusing to apologize is not conviction. A person does not get to rename abuse as the unfortunate shadow of excellence and expect everybody else to become grateful for it. The simplest distinction I have found is to ask who is being made to pay.

Arnold's training regime was extreme, but Arnold lifted the weights. Morrison woke before dawn, but she woke herself. The first and greatest cost of their ambition fell on them.

The moral problem changes when the ambitious person begins sending the bill to everybody else. When employees must absorb humiliation, a spouse must carry every abandoned responsibility, children must live beneath permanent neglect, teammates must accept violence, or less powerful people are bullied into silence, we are no longer discussing a harmlessly uneven person. We are discussing harm.

The see-saw is not a moral blank cheque; some weight crushes whoever is standing underneath it.

Steve Jobs could have demanded excellent work without degrading people. Newton could have defended his contribution without trying to destroy Leibniz. Michael Jordan needed high standards; he did not need every method he used to enforce them.

The fact that a weakness may grow from the same root as a strength does not make the weakness sacred. It simply means changing it may require more wisdom than shouting, "Be better."

The better questions are: Is this trait merely making the person less pleasant, or is it harming other people? Is it interfering with the work they themselves care about? Can they change the expression of the trait without cutting away its useful core? Can a team provide balance where one person cannot?

That last question matters. Jordan had Pippen. Jobs had people who could translate vision into engineering and survive the force of his personality. Great work is often not produced by one perfectly balanced person, but by several uneven people whose strengths cover one another's weaknesses.

Maybe we should stop demanding that every person become a complete team.

## We forget to turn the inspection inward

There is also a quiet arrogance in constantly auditing other people.

Society often demands a perfection from other people that none of us could survive being asked to demonstrate ourselves. We want the leader to be decisive and endlessly patient, the artist uncompromising and easygoing, the friend always available yet magnificently accomplished. We judge the whole person by the one quality missing from their arrangement, while asking that our own missing qualities be understood as context, fatigue, temperament, or a work in progress.

We look at the awkward genius and say, "Work on your social skills," as if we have finished working on courage, discipline, focus, honesty, fitness, money, patience, ambition, and every other place where our own lives are visibly underdeveloped.

We are extremely alert to the missing qualities in people who have something we admire. Perhaps that is because their imbalance comforts us. If we can focus on the founder's failed marriage, the artist's terrible finances, the athlete's ego, or the scientist's loneliness, then their excellence stops accusing us.

We can reduce the uncomfortable miracle of what they did to a reassuring sentence: "Yes, but look at everything wrong with them."

And there may be plenty wrong with them.

There is plenty wrong with us too. Ours is simply less visible because it has not been placed beside anything extraordinary.

That asymmetry is not moral seriousness. It is vanity dressed as discernment. The hallmark of wisdom is not blindness to flaws, but humility about the fact that flawlessness is not among the human options. Pragmatism begins when we stop comparing real people with imaginary people who pay no price for their virtues.

That is the part I keep returning to. Before I finish explaining how another person should become more complete, have I asked [what my own arrangement is producing](/blog/shut-up-act)? What is on the high side of my see-saw? What have I developed so well that it justifies the things I keep calling trade-offs?

It is easy to explain your failures as the price of your strengths when you have not actually built the strengths.

## Let people be uneven

You do not have to like every impressive person. You do not have to invite them to dinner. You do not have to work for them, marry them, defend them, or pretend their worst behaviour is charming because history may remember their name.

You should hold people accountable when they hurt others and tell the truth about the full person, especially when public worship tries to hide the damage. But the truth must run in both directions: do not use a person's weakness to erase their gift or their gift to erase their weakness. Do not turn every awkward edge into a moral failure, and do not assume the qualities you prefer are the qualities every life must optimize for.

Most of all, stop meeting an extraordinary but uneven human being and immediately redesigning them into someone more ordinary and more convenient.

Perhaps they should be kinder, listen more, learn to rest, apologize, communicate, or allow other people into the room. But perhaps we should also become more capable of encountering a person who is excellent at one thing without asking them to be excellent at being everything.

Life is a see-saw. Every strength places weight somewhere. Every choice leaves another life unlived. Every person is a strange arrangement of gifts, absences, disciplines, appetites, wounds, and limits.

Yin and yang offer a useful picture of this. Light and shadow do not merely sit beside each other; each carries a trace of the other. Human qualities often behave the same way. A strength can contain the seed of a weakness, and a limitation can create the conditions in which a gift becomes possible. Wisdom is learning which shadows must be accepted, which can be disciplined, and which cross the line into harm.

The goal is not to become perfectly balanced, because perfect balance may simply mean never putting enough weight anywhere to rise. The better goal is to know what your imbalance is for, take responsibility for who it hurts, and [build something worthy with the side you chose](/blog/agency-is-what-you-need).
